Pillar 02 · Acquisition

The hardware.

A custom EEG acquisition device built around a Raspberry Pi — from board bring-up to a hand-built electrode cap.

00 / the rig

Every component, on one table

The complete setup: an acquisition board, a microcomputer, a battery for safe electrical isolation, gold electrodes with conductive paste, a hand-built cap, and the skin-prep supplies that turned out to matter far more than expected.

01 / components

The signal chain, part by part

Follow the signal from scalp to computer: electrodes pick up microvolt brain activity, the PiEEG board amplifies and digitizes it, and the Raspberry Pi records and processes it — all powered by an isolated battery for safety.

Sensors

Gold-cup electrodes & paste

Gold-cup electrodes make electrical contact with the scalp. With conductive neurodiagnostic paste bridging cup to skin, they pick up the brain's faint electrical activity at specific 10–20 positions and carry it — via jumper wires — to the acquisition board. These are the sensors at the very front of the signal chain.

Analog front-end

PiEEG-16 board

A shield that seats directly on the Pi's 40-pin GPIO header. Its two ADS1299 chips amplify and digitize the microvolt-scale signals from up to 16 electrode channels (plus reference and bias), streaming them to the Pi over SPI. This is what turns faint scalp voltages into numbers the computer can read.

Compute

Raspberry Pi 5

The microcomputer that runs everything. It reads the digitized EEG over its GPIO pins, runs the recording software, and can host the signal processing and classification on-device. An established, affordable platform — the "full-circle" heart of the project.

Electrode holder

Custom electrode cap

A hand-built holder that positions each electrode at the correct 10–20 site and holds it in stable scalp contact. Built from a cotton beanie with grommets for firm, independent electrode seating and an elastic band to keep the reference and ground electrodes pressed in place. The part that took by far the most iteration to get right.

02 / the build

How it came together

1 · Sourcing — don't reinvent the wheel

The first decision was pragmatic: build around what already existed and was proven, rather than reinventing it. I already had a Raspberry Pi, so the PiEEG-16 shield — an established, research-cited board that mounts directly on it — was a natural fit. I also already had the exact power the board requires: a 5 V, 3–3.5 A, 10,000 mAh battery. Battery power isn't optional here — it electrically isolates the device from mains, which is a genuine safety requirement when electrodes are on your head.

2 · Board bring-up — does it read signal?

Before any electrodes, the goal was simply to prove the acquisition chain worked: mount the board, enable SPI, install the PiEEG software, and confirm the Pi could read data off all 16 channels. With inputs floating, the board correctly showed its noise floor — the "hello world" that confirmed the hardware was alive and communicating.

3 · The cap — the real battle

Getting clean signal off my own scalp was, by far, the hardest part. Early caps made from silicone swim caps were too slippery and never held electrodes snugly. A cotton beanie gripped better, but its closed knit fabric blocked access to the scalp — I couldn't part hair to reach skin through it. The breakthrough was grommets: enlarging the holes for scalp access and seating rubber grommets that grip each electrode independently, so adjusting one no longer disturbed the others. An elastic band held the reference and ground electrodes in stable contact. Across many sessions, the pattern was clear — mechanical stability and contact quality determine everything downstream.

The honest version: this took well over a dozen recording sessions. Most were noisy, drifted over time, or failed outright. Diagnosing why — and learning to change one variable at a time — was as much of the project as the signal processing itself.

4 · Results — promising and not

What worked: once the grommet cap was dialed in, the device captured my own alpha rhythm reliably and repeatably — the classic occipital signal that rises when you close your eyes. Detecting a real brain signal on hardware I built and debugged myself was the milestone the whole hardware effort was chasing. (The full analysis lives on the software page.)

What didn't: the ultimate goal — decoding imagined left- vs. right-hand movement — did not classify above chance on this setup. With only three motor-cortex channels, an unpracticed mental task, and a still-imperfect noise floor, that outcome is consistent with the literature: motor imagery is genuinely hard, and my own earlier study found most people don't produce cleanly classifiable motor imagery even on research-grade equipment. It's an honest result, and it points clearly at what future work would need — more channels, lower noise, and practice.
